For a long time, I believed intelligence was the main thing that mattered.

<!--more-->

Probably because everything around me said so. School. Tests. Rankings. Media. The quiet worship of IQ. The idea that smart people naturally win, and everyone else plays catch-up.

That belief was wrong.

Intelligence matters. But agency decides outcomes.

Agency is rarer. And more powerful.

Agency is the belief that you can move your life. That your mind belongs to you. That when something breaks, you don't freeze and wait for instructions. You act, adjust, and keep going.

Put simply, agency is what intelligence needs in order to work.


## why does intelligence get overrated?

We love intelligence because it feels clean, measurable, and safe.

An exam score tells you who's "good." A degree signals competence. A credential calms anxiety. You followed the path and checked the boxes. Agency does none of that.

Agency feels messy. Uncertain. Risky. It doesn't come with permission slips. That's why cultures often reward intelligence while quietly suppressing agency.

We educate for compliance more than initiative. We hire for resumes instead of judgment. We optimize for correctness instead of adaptability.

Then we act surprised when people freeze outside structured environments. You're basically an RL agent placed into a new environment without knowing how to explore.

## what does agency actually look like?

Agency has nothing to do with being loud or dominant.

High-agency people are often quiet. They don't need constant validation. They don't confuse credentials with capability. They see a problem and think, "I'll figure something out," and then they do.

One way to think about agency is this: be the subject of the sentence, not the object.

High-agency people respond. Redirect. Interfere. They don't simply react to circumstances. They shape them.

## agency survives the future

Everyone keeps saying most skills will become irrelevant in ten or twenty years.

Maybe that's true. Maybe it isn't. Either way, it misses the point.

High-agency people don't anchor their identity to a single skill. They know skills can be learned, abandoned, and rebuilt. They don't panic when industries change because they adapt faster than the changes themselves.

Replace their tools once, and they'll show up again with different tools but the same internal engine.

That's why agency compounds over time, while individual skills decay.

## why do many people never develop it?

Agency doesn't appear automatically.

If your environment rewarded obedience over curiosity, you probably learned to wait. If survival depended on approval, you learned conformity.

That doesn't make you weak. It means you were trained.

The problem is that conformity continues to feel like safety long after it stops being useful. You end up with beliefs you never questioned, opinions borrowed from culture, and goals chosen because they looked prestigious rather than meaningful.

That's not agency.

That's pre-installed software.

## conformity as the default state

Most people never consciously choose their operating system.

Beliefs come preloaded. Ideas feel true because they're repeated often enough. Opinions spread through imitation instead of investigation.

That's efficient for societies.

It's terrible for individuals.

Truth is usually found by people willing to question assumptions. Not through violent revolt, but through intellectual revolt. The refusal to outsource perception.

Agency begins where conformity ends.

## iteration without permission

Here's one of the clearest signs of agency.

High-agency people iterate without asking for permission.

They try. Observe. Adjust. Try again.

No committee approval. No perfect plan.

Most people act once. When it doesn't work, they retreat into comfort and call it realism.

Agency shows up in repetition.

Writing after nobody reads.

Building after something fails.

Learning after looking stupid.

Iteration filters out almost everyone.

## life as an experiment

Low-agency people treat life like a script.

High-agency people treat it like a laboratory.

They form hypotheses about their future. They test ideas in small ways. They expect failure to provide data, not damage their identity.

That mindset separates creators from spectators.

Whenever someone promises success through a formula, agency quietly disappears. You follow instructions. When the formula fails, you blame the system.

The experimental mindset reverses that.

You own the process, not just the outcome.

## believing in the difficult

Agency doesn't mean believing everything is possible.

It means believing difficult things are worth attempting.

Easy goals require little agency because you already know how to achieve them.

Impossible goals stop most people before they begin.

Difficult goals sit in the middle. They demand growth, skill acquisition, and patience.

Society often teaches people to mistake difficult for impossible. Once that belief takes hold, people spend years living lives they could have changed.

Agency interrupts that pattern.

## why does this matter now?

Modern life rewards passivity better than ever.

Endless feeds. Algorithmic recommendations. Outsourced decisions. Infinite distraction.

Agency requires friction. It requires deliberate choices. It requires deciding what matters before someone else decides for you.

That's uncomfortable.

That's why it's rare.

If there's one quality worth cultivating for the rest of your life, it's AGENCY.
